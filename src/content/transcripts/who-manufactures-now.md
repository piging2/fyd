## E07: Stockroom Tetris

## E08: The Intercom Voice

**NOLAN:** I have a second job inside this job. The second job has no chair and no name. The second job is the intercom.

**TRISH:** The voice of the store. The invisible man. God, if God worked retail and announced cleanups with the enthusiasm of a hostage reading a note.

**NOLAN:** You do not choose the intercom. The intercom chooses you. It chooses whoever is standing closest to the phone when the spill happens.

**TRISH:** Proximity is the entire hiring process. You were near a phone in a moment of crisis and now you are a broadcaster with a mop pending.

**NOLAN:** There is a button you hold. You hold it the whole time you talk. If you let go early, the store hears half an announcement and a click.

**TRISH:** The click of destiny. Attention shoppers, we are now closing in fif. Click. Fif what. Fif minutes. Fif dollars. Fif is not a unit, Nolan, but it is now, you made it one.

**NOLAN:** The first rule is to know what you are going to say before you press the button. I learned this rule by breaking it in front of two hundred people.

**TRISH:** Dead air. The most expensive sound in retail. A man holding a button, broadcasting his thinking face to the entire building, one um at a time.

**NOLAN:** The dead air has a texture. You can hear the fluorescent lights thinking. You can hear your own career changing shape.

**TRISH:** Somewhere in produce a man is holding a melon, listening to you breathe into the ceiling, wondering if the store has been taken over by a very calm ghost.

**NOLAN:** I have a voice I use. It is not my voice. My voice is this one. The intercom voice is my voice after it went to finishing school and dropped out.

**TRISH:** The voice puts on a blazer. You can hear the blazer. Attention shoppers becomes ATTENTION SHOPPERS, like the words got promoted and you are just the stairs they climbed.

**NOLAN:** The voice is slower. The voice is warmer. The voice believes in the store in a way I am not legally allowed to.

**TRISH:** The voice says our family of associates. Nolan. You have never called a single human in this building family without a gun to your head. The voice does it hourly. The voice is a liar in a blazer.

**NOLAN:** The cleaning announcements are the most common. Cleanup on aisle seven. The aisle changes. The tone never does.

**TRISH:** Cleanup on aisle seven, said like it is a wedding announcement. Two hearts, one mop, forever. Meanwhile the spill is achieving sentience and spreading into home goods.

**NOLAN:** Nobody hears the announcements. The announcements are weather. Shoppers shop through them the way you drive through rain with the wipers on.

**TRISH:** Total immunity. You could announce a fire, a flood, and a free car giveaway in one sentence and the building would hear none of it. The intercom is a tree falling in a forest that sells lawn chairs.

**NOLAN:** Except one. One announcement everybody hears, and it is always the one you mispronounce.

**TRISH:** The name. The name over the intercom is forever. Say it wrong once and it is carved into the ceiling tiles for a decade. The store remembers what the store was never asked to hear.

**NOLAN:** I announced a price drop on shrimp and said the price of shrimp. The sentence folded in the middle. People came to the register with shrimp and questions.

**TRISH:** A run on shrimp caused by grammar. The seafood department still tells the story. You moved forty pounds of shellfish with one broken sentence. Marketing could never.

**NOLAN:** There is an art to the call for backup. You do not say we are drowning. You say second register, please, like it is a dinner reservation.

**TRISH:** Second register, please. So calm. So civilized. Meanwhile the line is eleven carts deep and a man is aging in real time next to the gift cards.

**NOLAN:** The codes exist so shoppers do not panic. Code this, code that. The codes are a secret language everyone in the building already knows.

**TRISH:** The codes fool nobody. A toddler knows what the code means. The code means something fell, something spilled, or somebody is about to have a very public afternoon.

**NOLAN:** When I page the back room, the back room takes its time. The page travels forty feet. The response travels at geological speed.

**TRISH:** The stockroom hears the page and holds a small parliament about it. Should we answer. Shall we send a man. The raccoons out back answer pages faster and they do not even work here. Yet.

**NOLAN:** The phone by the register has a cord from another century. It tangles while you talk. By the end of an announcement I am wearing the cord like jewelry.

**TRISH:** The cord is a slow anaconda. It wraps the wrist, it wraps the receiver, and one day it will wrap the whole cashier and the store will just have a new statue.

**NOLAN:** The worst part is the echo of your own voice coming back off the far wall, one second late, disagreeing with you.

**TRISH:** You announce one thing and the building announces it back wrong. It is like arguing with yourself in public, except the building wins because the building is bigger and has more speakers.

**NOLAN:** The closing announcements are a different genre. Those are diplomacy. You are negotiating with a building full of people who do not want to leave.

**TRISH:** Good evening, the store will close in fifteen minutes, said sweetly, means get out, translated by a man too tired to start a war he would win by locking a door.

**NOLAN:** I do the fifteen minute, the ten minute, the five minute, and the final. Four announcements. Each one warmer. Each one a lie about my feelings.

**TRISH:** A countdown in a cardigan. And still, at zero minutes, there is a man in hardware discovering a hinge like it is the first hinge ever made by human hands.

**NOLAN:** The music cuts off before the final announcement. The silence is part of the message. The store exhales and the voice fills the exhale.

**TRISH:** The music dying is the loudest announcement of the day and you did not even have to hold the button. The building speaks fluent passive aggressive and the playlist is its first language.

**NOLAN:** One day the intercom played my page into the parking lot speakers at full volume. My voice, announcing a cleanup, to the entire county, at the size of weather.

**TRISH:** Cleanup on aisle seven, broadcast to three zip codes. Farmers heard it. Somewhere a man mopped his own kitchen out of respect. That is reach, Nolan. That is influence.

**NOLAN:** I have announced lost keys, lost carts, lost children of the cart variety. I have never once announced anything that was found.

**TRISH:** The intercom is a one way street for loss. Things go on the air missing and come back quietly, on foot, embarrassed, through the service desk. The voice gets none of the credit. The voice never does.

**TRISH:** The voice is a uniform you wear in your throat. You hang it up at night next to the suffering. Tomorrow the button will be there, the dead air will be there, and you will stand near the phone at exactly the wrong moment, because standing is your whole thing and the store knows it.

## E09: The Coupon Tribunal

**NOLAN:** The register is a courtroom. I did not apply for this. I was hired to scan groceries and somewhere along the way I was appointed to the bench.

**TRISH:** Judge Nolan, presiding over the court of small savings. Robe by apron. Gavel by scanner gun. All rise, the honor of forty cents is at stake.

**NOLAN:** The coupon arrives in many forms. There is the clean coupon, cut straight, in date, for a product that exists. The clean coupon is a unicorn and we treat it with reverence.

**TRISH:** And then there is the binder. You know the binder is coming before you see it. You hear it. Zipper teeth. Plastic sleeves. A filing system with handles and intent.

**NOLAN:** The binder is organized by aisle, by brand, and by what I can only describe as legal theory. There are tabs. There are sections. There is a table of contents.

**TRISH:** A table of contents for coupons. Somewhere a law library is missing its most dangerous mind. That binder has been to law school. That binder passed the bar of a grocery store.

**NOLAN:** Every coupon in the binder is presented one at a time, face up, like evidence. Exhibit A. A dollar off two. The two are in the cart. The dollar is demanded.

**TRISH:** And the register is the judge's brain. Beep means the law is satisfied. The other beep, the low one, means the law has objections and the courtroom holds its breath.

**NOLAN:** The low beep is the register clearing its throat. It means the coupon and the cart are in a disagreement I have to mediate with my face.

**TRISH:** Your face is the entire appeals process. Eyebrows up, we retry. Eyebrows down, the motion is denied and everybody in line three learns the ruling at the same time.

**NOLAN:** The expired coupon is the most common case before the court. Expired by a month is a tragedy. Expired by a year is archaeology. Expired by one day is a constitutional crisis.

**TRISH:** One day. One day is the defense that never dies. It was good yesterday, your honor. Yesterday it was a dollar. Today it is a war crime. The calendar is the real defendant here and the calendar never shows up to testify.

**NOLAN:** The register does not care about yesterday. The register lives in today with total commitment. The register is the only honest clock in the building.

**TRISH:** The register would convict its own mother on a date stamp. No mercy, no memory, no yesterday. Just beep or the low beep, justice or the throat clearing of justice.

**NOLAN:** Then there is the coupon for a product not in the cart. This is presented with total confidence. The coupon believes in itself completely.

**TRISH:** A coupon for detergent, presented over a cart containing no detergent, only vibes and canned beans. The argument is that the coupon should apply to the shopping in spirit. Spirit is not a barcode, but God bless the attempt.

**NOLAN:** The coupon that has been photocopied has a special energy. It comes in slightly gray, slightly crooked, like it has been faxed from 1994 for this exact transaction.

**TRISH:** A photocopy of a photocopy. Generational loss. The barcode looks like a zebra in fog. And still it scans sometimes, and when it scans, the whole line applauds the fog zebra.

**NOLAN:** The phone coupon is the new frontier. The phone is presented at an angle, brightness at zero, screen asleep, and then woken up to a photo of a coupon.

**TRISH:** A screenshot of a coupon is a rumor of a coupon. It is hearsay with a battery percentage. And the screenshot is always of a screen that was itself photographing another screen. Coupon inception. Savings all the way down.

**NOLAN:** The phone is passed across the counter like a sacred object. I hold it. The screen locks. We begin the ceremony again from the top.

**TRISH:** The unlock ritual. The phone locks, the face is apologized to, the phone is unlocked, the photo is found, the photo is zoomed, the barcode becomes four hundred pixels wide, and the scanner reads it like a poem and weeps beep.

**NOLAN:** Paper has one advantage. Paper never runs out of battery in the middle of its own argument.

**TRISH:** Paper is honest. Paper can be expired, it can be wrong, it can be for a store that closed in another state, but paper never needs a charger to state its case. Paper dies standing up.

**NOLAN:** The store in another state is a real category. A coupon from a sister store, a cousin store, a store related by marriage to our store.

**TRISH:** Genealogy coupons. This coupon is valid at your uncle's location three states away, and yet here it stands, at my register, demanding citizenship. The court recognizes the document. The court cannot honor it. The court is devastated.

**NOLAN:** When two coupons want the same item, we hold the doubling hearing. Can they stack. Can they combine. The register decides, and the register is a strict constructionist.

**TRISH:** One item, one discount, one law. The register reads the fine print so you do not have to, and the fine print is written in a font designed by antagonists.

**NOLAN:** The fine print is printed at a size meant to be read by ants with library cards. Limit four. Excludes everything you love. Void where prohibited and three other places.

**TRISH:** Void in three other places. Which places. The coupon will not say. The coupon is a spy. The coupon has clearance beyond its own knowledge and it will die before it talks.

**NOLAN:** Sometimes the coupon wins. Clean, in date, product in the cart, beep like a bell. The whole line feels it. A small parade breaks out in the cereal aisle of the soul.

**TRISH:** And sometimes there is the manager override, the pardon power. The key turns, the register forgives, and the dollar is granted by executive mercy. You watch a dollar get pardoned like a turkey in November.

**NOLAN:** I am not allowed to pardon. I can only recommend. My recommendation carries the weight of a man who has been standing for six hours.

**TRISH:** Six hours of standing, and let the record show the standing. This court is in session wherever you are parked, and you are parked at register four until the building says otherwise. Suffering is your jurisdiction.

**NOLAN:** The line behind the tribunal grows. The line is the jury. The jury has groceries melting in solidarity and opinions forming like weather.

**TRISH:** Ice cream waits for no verdict. There is a tub out there softening through the entire trial, and when the ruling lands, win or lose, somebody is eating soup through a cone tonight.

**NOLAN:** There is also the coupon that scans perfectly and takes off eleven cents. Eleven cents, after a presentation that lasted longer than some weddings.

**TRISH:** Eleven cents, fought for like a border town. Speeches were made. The binder was opened to page forty. And the republic gained eleven cents, which will be spent immediately on nothing, proudly.

**NOLAN:** The victory is the value. I have learned this. The register keeps score and the score is the point.

**TRISH:** The coupon is a sport. The cart is the season. Some people golf, some people fish, and some people are undefeated at register four with a binder and a laminated strategy.

**NOLAN:** The laminated coupon deserves its own paragraph in the record. Somebody, at home, with a machine, sealed a coupon in plastic so it could serve forever.

**TRISH:** Immortality for a discount. That coupon will outlive the store, the brand, and possibly the concept of soup. It will be found in a drawer in fifty years, still valid in spirit, still demanding its dollar like a tiny paper ghost.

**NOLAN:** The handwritten coupon amendment is where I draw my one line. Somebody has improved a coupon with a pen. The terms have been edited by the plaintiff.

**TRISH:** A coupon with corrections. Was a dollar, is now, in confident handwriting, five dollars and a free ham. The pen is mightier than the barcode, your honor, but the court remains unmoved.

**NOLAN:** The register reads barcodes, not intentions. I say this out loud sometimes. It is my one piece of case law.

**TRISH:** Register reads barcodes, not intentions. Put it on the courthouse wall. Put it on your tombstone. It is the truest sentence ever spoken at register four and it has ended more arguments than the manager.

**NOLAN:** Kids watch the tribunal like it is a show. In a way it is a show. It has stakes, costumes, and a document with tabs.

**TRISH:** Better than television. Live law, real dollars, a judge in an apron, and a gavel that also weighs bananas. The network is sleeping on this. The binder alone could carry a season.

**NOLAN:** When the last coupon clears and the receipt prints, the receipt is the written opinion of the court. It is four feet long and nobody reads it.

**TRISH:** The longest document in the building per dollar decided. War and Peace, itemized. Somewhere in that scroll is the truth about the eleven cents, recorded forever, cited by no one.

**NOLAN:** The two for coupon is a geometry problem wearing a legal costume. Buy two, get one. There are two in the cart. The third is demanded as a citizen of the deal.

**TRISH:** The third item is not in the cart but it is in the argument, and the argument gives it a little hat and a name. The register counts carts, not arguments. The register is heartless and correct, my two favorite qualities in a machine.

**NOLAN:** And the rain check precedent gets cited in coupon court too. A price from last week, a promise in writing, carried in like a treaty signed by both nations.

**TRISH:** The rain check is a passport from the past. It gets waved at borders. Sometimes the border honors it and sometimes the border is a nineteen year old with a scanner and no fear.

**NOLAN:** The hardest cases are the honest mistakes. A coupon clipped in good faith for the wrong size. The court wants mercy. The register wants specifics.

**TRISH:** And that is the whole tragedy of the bench, your honor. You have a heart and the machine does not, and the line is watching to see which one of you is contagious.

**NOLAN:** Some days mercy wins on the manager's key. Some days the date wins. The docket never empties either way.

**TRISH:** Night moves. The aisle performs its encore for an audience of security cameras, and the footage, if anyone ever watched it, would look haunted by legumes.

**NOLAN:** One pea reached the front door mat on day four. Four departments, two thresholds, one working vacation from the freezer it was sentenced to.

**TRISH:** It ended free and soft on a welcome mat. There are worse biographies in this building, and some of them are printed on name tags.

**NOLAN:** The broom still gets a look when it passes frozen. Nobody trusts it in that aisle. It has a record now, and the record is green.

**TRISH:** A tool on probation. It sweeps the other aisles like nothing happened, but frozen remembers, and so does every pea still in reserve.

**NOLAN:** I counted once, roughly, from the recovered bins and the trail math. We got most of them. Most is a word that does a lot of night work in that sentence.

**TRISH:** Most. The store's favorite measurement. Most of the peas, most of the tape, most of the truth, and the rest is out there rolling toward a welcome mat.

**NOLAN:** Somewhere between the spill and the mop, the aisle learned its lesson, which is that it will learn it again on the next restock night, same bag, same seam.

**TRISH:** Landslides are scheduled weather here, with a barcode. The bag splits, the hail falls, the floor skates, the broom lies, and the last pea clocks out three days after everybody else.

**NOLAN:** I keep a hand under every bag now. Seam up, weight low, like carrying a sleeping animal that dreams of escape velocity.

**TRISH:** And the floor keeps its one pea in reserve, rolling somewhere behind the shelves, waiting for a quiet minute and a clean pair of shoes. Respect the reserve. It outranks us both in patience.

## E16: The Scanner Gun and the Holster Problem

**NOLAN:** The scanner gun is issued like a sidearm and treated like a celebrity. It has a cradle, a name in the system, and a battery that lies to you in percentages.

**TRISH:** Ninety percent at nine in the morning. Forty by lunch. Dead by produce. That battery defects by produce. It joins the other side an hour before the count ends.

**NOLAN:** The holster is the scandal. It clips to the belt at an angle the designer never tested on a human who bends. Every deep bend is a small betrayal.

**TRISH:** You squat for the bottom shelf count and the gun slides out like it is escaping custody. It hits the floor, spins once, and points at the canned goods like it is making an accusation.

**NOLAN:** It has survived four hundred drops. The screen is cracked in the corner in a pattern like a river delta. It scans through the crack without complaint.

**TRISH:** The crack is load bearing now. Maintenance says a new screen costs more than the gun. So the river stays, and every count flows through it.

**NOLAN:** The beep is the whole language. One beep means yes. Two beeps mean no. A long flat tone means the gun has seen something it refuses to discuss.

**TRISH:** The beeps are verdicts. Short beep: counted. Double beep: try again, amateur. Long tone: the item has no record, no history, no legal existence. Congratulations, you are holding a ghost.

**NOLAN:** Trigger discipline is a real curriculum here. You do not fan the trigger. You do not scan the same case six times because the beep was satisfying.

**TRISH:** Six scans of one case of beans is how a store orders a bean mountain in March. The gun does not know you were vibing. The gun reports what you confessed.

**NOLAN:** The laser is theatrical at range. It will read a barcode through a freezer door, across an aisle, off a box you are not looking at.

**TRISH:** It scans things behind you. It scans things in the next aisle. It has scanned a truck through the dock door glass. The gun is ambitious without supervision.

**NOLAN:** And then there is the one barcode that will not read. Every inventory has one. A crumpled label on a dented can, the code half sacrificed to a box cutter.

**TRISH:** That barcode is a sworn enemy. You angle it. You flatten it. You hold it to the light like a ransom note. The gun looks at it and declines, politely, four hundred times.

**NOLAN:** You type the number in by hand in the end. Twelve digits, one finger, while the gun hangs at your hip getting its strength back.

**TRISH:** Manual entry. The lowest technology in the building performed by the most expensive employee per hour: a human who has been defeated by a sticker.

**NOLAN:** The laser disappears in daylight. Near the front windows you are aiming on faith. You sweep the gun in a slow arc and listen for the beep like sonar.

**TRISH:** A grown man sweeping groceries for mines. The beep is the only proof the item exists. Half the front end inventory is confirmed by sound, like bats with a payroll department.

**NOLAN:** The charging bank is a dormitory with politics. Eight slots, five working chargers, and a note in marker that has been there so long the marker has faded into folklore.

**TRISH:** The note says do not take gun two. It does not say why. Nobody asks why anymore. The note has outlasted three managers. Gun two is either haunted or promoted, and either way it does not work nights.

**NOLAN:** The trigger finger develops its own complaint. Two hours of counting and the finger keeps clicking at rest, in the air, like it is counting things the eyes have not approved.

**TRISH:** Phantom scans. You will click at a menu, at a steering wheel, at your own reflection. The gun trains the hand and then goes back in the cradle and leaves the hand out there, still working.

**NOLAN:** The calibration sticker on the side expired in a year nobody on the schedule remembers. The gun has been legally approximate for a long time.

**TRISH:** Approximate is the house style. The scale is approximate, the clock in the break room is approximate, and the gun is approximate with a serial number. The count is a novel the store writes about itself every quarter.

**NOLAN:** Once, the gun went through the wash of a spilled mop bucket at the dock. It came back on in twenty minutes, beeping weakly, like it had seen the other side and found it unstocked.

**TRISH:** It died counting and came back counting. Say what you want about the equipment budget, that is a work ethic. The gun has been dropped, frozen, soaked, and lied to by its own battery, and it still shows up to accuse soup.

**NOLAN:** The cradle is the other war. Half the guns do not seat right. You dock it, the light stays red, you wiggle it, the light thinks about it.

**TRISH:** The wiggle is a handshake only veterans know. Too soft and it sulks. Too hard and you have committed violence against county property. There is a click. You dream about the click.

**NOLAN:** Sign out is a ledger and a promise. Name, gun number, time. The gun comes back when it comes back. The ledger has entries that read like missing persons reports.

**TRISH:** Gun seven has been signed out since a Tuesday in another season. Somewhere there is a retired vest with gun seven in the pocket, still holding a charge out of spite.

**NOLAN:** The strap saves it weekly. It dangles, it swings, it knocks against the shelving in a rhythm the whole back room can identify by ear.

**TRISH:** That knock is a status report. Clank means counting. Double clank means the gun is out of the holster again and morale is falling. Silence means someone is typing twelve digits and questioning everything.

**NOLAN:** Cold kills it fastest. Twenty minutes counting in the freezer and the screen slows down like it is thinking in another language.

**TRISH:** The numbers crawl out one digit at a time, like the gun is apologizing in Morse. You warm it inside your vest like a baby bird and it forgives you in installments.

**NOLAN:** The count wand attachment exists somewhere. Nobody has seen it since it was photographed for the asset register. The photograph is very clear. The wand is a rumor with a serial number.

**TRISH:** An asset register full of ghosts. Half the equipment in this store exists the way Atlantis exists: well documented, frequently cited, never once located.

**NOLAN:** You develop a draw. Count long enough and your hand goes to your hip before your eyes find the shelf. The body files the motion under survival.

**TRISH:** Cashiers hear the draw in your shoulders. You reach for that gun at family dinners. You have tried to scan a menu. The menu had no barcode. The gun was disappointed in both of you.

**NOLAN:** The screen asks questions with no good answers. Quantity on hand: zero. The shelf in front of you holds forty. You are asked to agree with the zero in writing.

**TRISH:** The system does not want the truth. The system wants a witness. You stand there holding forty yogurts, and the database swears the shelf is a rumor. Somebody has to perjure themselves, and it is always the person holding the gun.

**NOLAN:** Discrepancies go in a note field with a character limit written by an optimist. Twelve characters to explain a pallet that does not exist on paper and does exist in the meat aisle.

**TRISH:** Twelve characters. I once explained a missing pallet in twelve characters and it read like a ransom demand from a very terse kidnapper. The office framed it. The pallet never surfaced.

**NOLAN:** The gun also keeps secrets. Scan a case wrong and it remembers your error at full volume in the middle of a quiet row of night stockers.

**TRISH:** The error tone carries. It has a range like a church bell. Three aisles of professionals look up from their boxes to watch you be wrong in surround sound.

**NOLAN:** Battery swap mid count is surgery. You hold the count in the gun's memory, the battery in your teeth, and your place in the aisle in your soul.

**TRISH:** One sneeze erases a freezer section. That is the whole database policy here: a five thousand dollar count held together by a man not sneezing near cold cuts.

**NOLAN:** The new guns arrive with a firmware voice that chirps confirmation in a tone so cheerful it feels like mockery at hour nine.

**TRISH:** The old guns grunt. The new guns celebrate. Neither one helps you lift the case. Cheerfulness is the one feature that never needs charging, and it is the first one you want to throw into the compactor.

**NOLAN:** There is a etiquette about whose gun is whose. You do not take the good gun from the charging bank without leaving your dying one in its exact spot, charging, as tribute.

**TRISH:** Gun politics is the real org chart. Titles lie. The charging bank tells the truth: whoever gets gun two, the one with the honest battery, runs this store, and everyone over the age of nineteen knows it.

**NOLAN:** The holster problem is formally unsolved. Three holster generations in the supply closet, each defeated by the same belt, the same bend, the same floor.

**TRISH:** Generation one dropped it forward. Generation two dropped it sideways. Generation three has a strap, and the strap is where dignity goes to be unclipped in public. The floor is undefeated. The floor is the champion of this weight class.

**NOLAN:** You learn to catch it with your knee. A soft knee catch, no look, while both hands hold a case of soup. It is the most athletic thing that happens in this building.

**TRISH:** Grocery ballet. No audience, no applause, just a man saving government grade electronics with his kneecap because a belt clip lost an argument with gravity for the nine hundredth time.

**NOLAN:** The holster wears the belt out before the belt wears out the holster. Every belt on the closing crew has a pale stripe where the clip rides, a tan line of office.

**TRISH:** The pale stripe is a rank insignia. New hires have clean belts. Veterans have the stripe, the knee catch, and a favorite gun they will deny having in front of the office. Feudalism, but with chargers.

**NOLAN:** Rain counts at the dock are the gun's final exam. Wet barcodes, wet hands, a wet screen fogging, and the truck driver holding a clipboard like none of this concerns the history of paper.

**TRISH:** Paper gets the umbrella. The gun gets the weather. Priorities, printed in triplicate. You scan forty cases of drinks in the rain and the gun beeps through it, offended but employed.

**NOLAN:** The screen brightness is a morale setting. Full bright drains the battery by dinner. Dim saves the charge and costs you squinting in a freezer at hour eight, reading numbers like tea leaves.

**TRISH:** Every setting is a trade with the devil, and the devil works in the electronics department. You pick your poison, bright and dead or dim and blind, and either way the yogurt gets counted by a man making theology out of a battery icon.

**NOLAN:** The spare gun in the office is a decoy. It powers on, it beeps, it has never once held a charge past the hour. It exists to be handed to the new person as a lesson.

**TRISH:** The decoy gun is hazing with a warranty. You learn the store's true curriculum from that gun: the equipment is a rumor, the count is a story, and the only reliable instrument in the building is the expression on the face of the person who counted before you.

**NOLAN:** And yet the count gets done. Every quarter, every gun, every bent holster, and the shelves give up their numbers one beep at a time.

**TRISH:** One beep at a time is the only honest speed this building has. The gun is a diva, the holster is a traitor, the battery is a fiction writer, and the count still lands, because a tired human with cold hands refuses to be outlasted by office equipment with a laser.

**NOLAN:** At the end of the count the numbers go up the wire and the office pretends the shelves have been known all along.

**TRISH:** The report says the store contains what the paper says it contains. The shelves, holding their actual soup in actual rows, accept the compliment and say nothing. They have tenure. Paper does not.

## E17: The Closing Announcement Voice

**NOLAN:** The closing announcements start an hour out, in a voice the store teaches you and nobody remembers learning. It is a voice from a higher, calmer store.

**TRISH:** The Announcements Voice. It has no region, no fatigue, and no history of being yelled at about a coupon. It is the voice of a store where the carts roll straight and the lights never hum.

**NOLAN:** First call is informational. Shoppers, the store will be closing in one hour. It is weather reporting. A front is coming, no need to move yet.

**TRISH:** One hour warning is pure courtesy. Nobody in the history of retail has ever hurried at sixty minutes. It is a rumor read aloud by a man holding a phone that also pages the back room wrong.

**NOLAN:** By forty five, the voice starts doing math out loud. Please bring your final selections to the front. Final is a lie we tell kindly. There will be more selections.

**TRISH:** Final selections. The store says final the way a parent says last call for snacks. Everybody in the building, including the voice, knows there is a man in hardware beginning a project at forty three minutes to close.

**NOLAN:** The shoppers do not leave in waves. They evaporate unevenly, like weather clearing off a map one county at a time.

**TRISH:** Weather is exactly right. Aisle six is clear and sunny. Aisle nine has a stationary front with a full cart and a phone call. You do not rush weather. You forecast around it and lock up the patio furniture.

**NOLAN:** Thirty minutes takes the voice up a register. It starts blessing people. Thank you for shopping with us, in a tone that has stopped being information.

**TRISH:** The blessing phase. The voice thanks the building for shopping with us, thanks the air for shopping with us. The grander the voice gets, the emptier the store, like it is saving its cathedral voice for the pigeons.

**NOLAN:** At fifteen, I catch myself announcing to eleven people like I am closing a national park. The store will be closing in fifteen minutes. The gates, in this case, are a sliding door with a mat.

**TRISH:** Fifteen minutes is where the voice puts on the robe. A keeper of a closing monument takes the microphone. Please conclude your visit. Conclude. The bread aisle has never been spoken to like that in its life.

**NOLAN:** There is always one shopper who treats the countdown as a personal broadcast and speeds up exactly none.

**TRISH:** The countdown immune. They hear fifteen minutes and start comparing two kinds of rice like the announcement was a starting gun for a very slow, very important race only they entered.

**NOLAN:** The announcements have a script for weather, a script for holidays nobody is allowed to decorate for yet, and a script for the parking lot lights being repaired.

**TRISH:** A script for every occasion except the truth. There is no approved wording for the store is closing because the crew has been standing since morning and their feet have filed a joint resignation. So the voice blesses the rice comparison instead.

**NOLAN:** You hear your own announcement while you are in the aisle, which is a strange haunting. The voice is calm and above you, and below it you are sweating through a restock.

**TRISH:** Being haunted by your own better self is a retail benefit no one lists. The ceiling version of you never hurries, never hurts, never once says the wrong name over the system. The floor version is doing penance in both of your names.

**NOLAN:** The microphone technique matters. Too close and the voice booms like weather. Too far and it arrives as a rumor from a neighboring store.

**TRISH:** There is a distance, learned by bruises, where the voice turns grand. Every veteran finds it. Every new hire broadcasts their first call either as a thunder god or as a man whispering into a soup can across town.

**NOLAN:** The dead spots are part of the liturgy. Over the freezer doors the announcements arrive as pure vowels. Shoppers nod at the vowels and keep shopping, which is the correct response to most of the script.

**TRISH:** Vowel service. The store closes in fifteen minutes, and the freezer aisle receives it as a soothing hum with consonants removed. In a way the freezers get the honest version: tone without content, exactly what the announcement is by then.

**NOLAN:** Each department edits the call in their head. Bakery hears bring your selections and thinks about the racks. The front end hears it and starts counting the minutes in carts.

**TRISH:** One voice, nine translations. It is the only document the whole store reads at the same time, and every department reads a different one. The announcement is a mirror with a deadline.

**NOLAN:** The voice has to stay kind while the feet start hurting. That is the discipline of it. The nicer the announcement, the worse the arches. It is a conversion the store runs for free.

**TRISH:** Kindness per decibel, paid for in arch damage. The voice floats over the store like a spa commercial while below it a man stands on concrete doing penance through his shoes. Dual broadcast. Always.

**NOLAN:** Ten minutes is the last honest call. After ten, the announcements become pure ceremony, addressed to the staff disguised as shoppers.

**TRISH:** Ten minutes is the truth window. Everything after that is liturgy for the crew: we are almost out, keep your faces arranged, do not let the voice crack where the last four people can hear it.

**NOLAN:** Someone always asks if we are really closing, like the announcements are a negotiating position.

**TRISH:** The store has announced its closing eleven times with increasing grandeur and a straight face, and still the question arrives every night, same wording, fresh wonder. Yes. The darkness scheduled for ten o'clock is not a bluff.

**NOLAN:** The deli hears the calls differently. Over there the countdown is a workload. Every announcement closes a case they have to scrub.

**TRISH:** Department physics. At produce, closing is a countdown. At the deli, closing is a debt coming due in bleach. Same voice in the ceiling, two entirely different religions underneath it.

**NOLAN:** Five minutes gets read slower. Enunciated. Like the voice is landing a plane it personally owns.

**TRISH:** Five minutes, full pilot voice. The store will be closing in five. Minutes. Each word set down gently like it is made of glass, addressed to an audience of nine humans and one man reading greeting cards at speed.

**NOLAN:** The greeting card reader is a closing institution. He arrives in the last hour the way certain birds arrive in a season. He reads twelve cards and buys one, or none, and leaves a better person.

**TRISH:** Do not rush the card man. The card man is load bearing. A closing without a card man reading about sympathy at nine fifty is a closing that has not been properly witnessed.

**NOLAN:** At two minutes the voice drops the store name into the announcement, which is the formal signature. Thank you for shopping at your neighborhood store. Saying the name means it is over.

**TRISH:** The name is the seal on the document. Before the name, it is weather. After the name, it is law. The whole crew feels it in their knees when the name lands.

**NOLAN:** And then there is the final call, after close, made to a store with nobody in it. I make it anyway. Every night. It feels wrong to skip it.

**TRISH:** The final call to the empty building is the purest one. No audience, no negotiation, just the voice informing the shelves that they are closed. The shelves, to their credit, receive the news like professionals.

**NOLAN:** The microphone has a weight by then. An hour of announcements lives in your throat. You hang it up and your normal voice comes back smaller, like it was behind the big one in line.

**TRISH:** The two voices have a custody arrangement. The big voice gets the ceiling and the last hour. Your voice gets the parking lot, the ride home, and apologizing to your own feet.

**NOLAN:** New hires always laugh the first time they hear their own announcement played back by the ceiling. It does not sound like them. It sounds like a man the store invented.

**TRISH:** The store invents one announcer per clerk, same man, different throats. He is calm, he is grand, he has never once stood in a freezer counting yogurt with a dying gun. We are all just his local representatives.

**NOLAN:** The worst night is when the system loops a stale announcement. The store will be closing in one hour, read to a store that closed twenty minutes ago, at full confidence.

**TRISH:** The ceiling lying with total conviction is the store's final form. Even the building does not know what time it is by the end. It just knows the voice, and the voice says an hour, forever.

**NOLAN:** Rain nights, the announcements get an addition. Please drive safely. It is the only sentence in the script anybody wrote with their whole heart.

**TRISH:** One honest line in the liturgy and it shows. The voice drops half a register on drive safely, like the announcer steps out from behind the store for one second and is just a person telling people to get home.

**NOLAN:** The register lines hear the countdown as a starting pistol for confessions. Every last shopper arrives at the belt carrying one item they forgot to mention to themselves all hour.

**TRISH:** The forgot item sprints are the closing ceremony nobody scheduled. A man remembers batteries at two minutes like the knowledge was delivered by the voice itself. The voice takes no credit. The voice has moved on to blessing the doors.

**NOLAN:** There is a version of the call for the last shopper specifically, though it is never scripted that way. You soften it. You aim it. Please make your way to the front, in the direction of one remaining human and twelve dark aisles.

**TRISH:** A shepherd's announcement. One sheep, addressed as a crowd, out of mercy. Nobody should have to hear a building say their name in the plural of one. So the voice keeps saying shoppers, and the one shopper carries the title for the whole species.

**NOLAN:** Snow nights the voice adds the lot warning twice. The second reading is not in the script. The second reading is the announcer remembering the hill on the access road.

**TRISH:** The script is a fence. The second reading is a person leaning on the fence, looking out at the weather, deciding the fence could use a gate. Every crew knows which announcers double the warning. They are the same ones who count the lot twice.

**NOLAN:** My voice does the thing where it gets formal when I am tired. The more exhausted the hour, the more the announcements sound like a state occasion.

**TRISH:** Fatigue in a sash and medals. The crew can read your tiredness off the ceiling like a gauge. When the voice starts addressing the store as this fine establishment, everyone out there knows the announcer is running on fumes and protocol.

**NOLAN:** The closing call is the only announcement the staff applauds, quietly, in the form of moving faster.

**TRISH:** Applause, store style: no hands, just velocity. The voice says the store is now closed and nine people accelerate like the sentence handed them back their bodies. It is the best review the announcements ever get, and it is never in writing.

**NOLAN:** Then the lights do their half dim, the doors lock on their timer, and the ceiling rests. The quiet after the last announcement has a texture. It is the first true thing all hour.

**TRISH:** Silence, store brand. The announcements end, the hum keeps its own counsel, and eleven employees stand in the sudden weather of a closed building, waiting for their feet to file the report their voices have been reading around all night.

## E18: The Last Corral Siege

**NOLAN:** The last roundup of the night is a different job from the afternoon one. The afternoon is retrieval. The night is a siege, conducted slowly, in the cold, against carts that have had all day to dig in.

**TRISH:** Daytime carts get left. Nighttime carts hide. There is a difference and your freezing hands learn it by the second week. A left cart is an accident. A hidden cart has a plan and three exits.

**NOLAN:** The lot changes at night. The far corners stop being parking and become territory. The last carts are never near the corrals. They are at the edges, by the tree line, under the one dead lamp.

**TRISH:** The dead lamp corner is the capital of the resistance. Carts migrate there across the whole day, one small abandonment at a time, and by closing there is a government out there in the dark with its own weather.

**NOLAN:** You can hear them before you see them. A loose wheel ticking in the wind. The lot at night has a sound track and it is all small metal deciding whether to hold still.

**TRISH:** The ticking is a taunt. It stops when you walk toward it. You are being outwaited by inventory. There is no training module for losing a staring contest to a cart in the dark, and there should be.

**NOLAN:** The cold changes the physics of the job. Bare hands on cold steel at closing time is a sensation with a long memory. Gloves live somewhere. Nobody has ever found the gloves during the roundup.

**TRISH:** The gloves are with the count wand and the spare caster, in the closet of documented rumors. So it is skin on frozen handle, and the handle keeps the change.

**NOLAN:** A train of carts at night behaves worse. The links stiffen. The whole line wants to fold at the first turn like it has somewhere better to be.

**TRISH:** A stiff cart train in the dark is a metal snake with opinions about direction. It will jackknife at the speed bump and shed its last two carts like a tail it can regrow. Somehow it always sheds them uphill of you.

**NOLAN:** The speed bumps run the night lot like checkpoints. Every crossing costs momentum, and momentum is the whole economy out there.

**TRISH:** Rich people have toll roads. We have a row of sleeping yellow humps that tax every cart train in effort and dignity. Pay at every bump. Exact change only. The change is your lower back.

**NOLAN:** One cart tonight was all the way at the neighboring lot, past the hedge, parked neatly at their corral like it had changed stores.

**TRISH:** Defection. Clean, orderly, parked with intent. That cart emigrated. It is standing in a foreign corral right now telling the other carts the grass is the same and the lamps all work.

**NOLAN:** Another one was behind the dumpster enclosure, where the raccoons hold their ground. I retrieved it at a diplomatic pace.

**TRISH:** A note on that retrieval: the parliament allows passage at night the way a border allows weather. Slowly, visibly, and with the understanding that the bakery bags are a matter for another evening. Nobody signed anything. The cart came home. Diplomacy is just retrieval with witnesses.

**NOLAN:** The carts wedge in winter. Not tonight, but the season is written on the lot in old scars. You learn the lot's cold history from the marks on the asphalt.

**TRISH:** Every lot keeps its diary in scratches. Here is where the train jackknifed in January. Here is where a man pushed a frozen line until the line won. The asphalt remembers what the schedule forgets.

**NOLAN:** The return bay lights time out while you are still wrestling the train in. Twice a night the lot goes dark mid push, and you finish the last ten feet by memory and sound.

**TRISH:** Working by sound is the last skill. The ticking, the rattle, the one bad bearing in the train singing its little song. You steer a hundred pounds of steel through a blackout by ear, like a man parking a piano.

**NOLAN:** There is always one cart accounted for nowhere. The count says a number, the corrals say a smaller number, and the difference is standing somewhere being scenery.

**TRISH:** The missing cart is never lost. It is placed. Someone with a long memory rolls it to the exact spot where the cameras argue with each other, and it stands there being a parking lot decoration with plausible deniability.

**NOLAN:** The flashlight is the night crew's second tool and first confession. Sweeping the far rows with a light means the eyes have officially resigned from the search.

**TRISH:** The flashlight finds everything except carts first. Raccoon eyes at the tree line, returning the beam with interest. A lost glove from a month with a different temperature. The lot keeps a lost and found nobody is brave enough to claim from.

**NOLAN:** The parliament watches the siege from the enclosure wall some nights, in a row, like season ticket holders.

**TRISH:** Spectators with seniority. They do not interfere with the roundup. Interference is scheduled, negotiated, and involves the bakery. The siege they merely review, and their reviews are written in posture: unimpressed, with one paw of respect for the gutter rescue.

**NOLAN:** Fog is the siege's hardest weather. The lot shrinks to ten feet in every direction and the carts become rumors with wheels.

**TRISH:** Retrieval by rumor. You push into the fog and meet your own cart train coming out of it like a ship. Every far cart that night gets found twice: once by the light, once by your shin meeting it in the gray.

**NOLAN:** The hands learn the job's arithmetic by January of the soul: one train is pride, two trains is strength, three trains is a noise complaint from your own spine.

**TRISH:** Nobody runs three trains at night on this crew. The night has a constitution and the first article is the spine. Two trains, slow turns, and the long way around the bumps, because the siege is won by whoever can still bend down tomorrow.

**NOLAN:** A cart with a wobbling caster at night sounds twice its size. The echo off the storefront gives every deserter an army.

**TRISH:** Echo is the lot's public address system after close. One loose wheel reads as a battalion. You walk toward the sound prepared for a regiment and find one small cart with a rattle, standing in the open like it wanted to be caught dramatically.

**NOLAN:** I found tonight's behind the propane cage, which is not a place a cart goes. That is a place a cart is put.

**TRISH:** Behind the propane cage is a message in a bottle. Somebody on the morning crew has feelings about the night crew, expressed in cart placement. It is the store's oldest correspondence. Nobody replies. Everybody reads.

**NOLAN:** The wind works the lot like an employee. It takes the light carts first. By the time you reach the far row, the wind has rearranged your assignment.

**TRISH:** The wind is the only staff member who does the roundup without being asked, and it does it wrong, for spite, in the direction of the street. Management will not discipline the wind. The wind has seniority and no fixed address.

**NOLAN:** Near the street is the hard boundary of the job. A cart in the gutter by the road is a rescue, and you do it fast, with the train parked and the hazard of the whole thing in your shoulders.

**TRISH:** The gutter cart is the night lot's final exam. Traffic, cold, a cart with its nose in the road like it was trying to flag a ride out of retail forever. You bring it home. They always come home. That is the rule the night runs on.

**NOLAN:** The corral at night fills in the wrong order. Tired hands park tired carts, and the nesting goes crooked, so the last train in has to be negotiated like a treaty.

**TRISH:** Crooked nesting at midnight is the shift signing its name. Straight rows are a morning virtue. The night signs in cursive, at an angle, with three carts sharing one slot like roommates.

**NOLAN:** There is a sound a full corral makes when the last cart clicks home. One clean note. It is the only part of closing that feels finished when it happens.

## E20: The Raccoon Treaty

**NOLAN:** The final trash run of the night is a signing ceremony with bags, and both governments know the way to the table.

**TRISH:** Twelve sessions of diplomacy led to this dock. Earlier access, warmer tribute, two bags on the table and one lid in dispute. Tonight the parliament signs, or it pretends to sign so beautifully that the difference retires.

**NOLAN:** I prepared the documents. The documents are one flattened cardboard box and a marker that only works when it feels respected.

**TRISH:** The treaty of the dock, drafted on the store's own cardboard in letters large enough for the fence to read from its bench. International law has been written on less. It has rarely been written on anything more absorbent.

**NOLAN:** The Foreman arrived first, alone, ahead of the session, and walked the dumpster once before taking his seat. An inspection before a signing. He wanted the record clean.

**TRISH:** He wanted the record his. He has built a career on the distance between clean and his. A statesman arrives early to a treaty the way weather arrives early to a picnic. To be already true when the others show up.

**NOLAN:** The terms were negotiated across two sessions and a dozen bags. Tonight was ratification. Article one is the tribute. Two bags a run. Bakery fixed. Produce variable.

**TRISH:** Ratified by appetite. The bakery bag is the fixed payment, sacred and scheduled. The produce bag rides the market of bent greens and soft fruit. The parliament invented variable tribute and then ate the prototype for the archives.

**NOLAN:** Article two is the hour. The second run moves earlier in the evening, while the bakery bags are still warm from the day they failed to sell in.

**TRISH:** Warm tribute at an earlier table. Your finest bargain, struck at the second negotiation with a leaking bag in your hand. Somewhere a trade minister is still taking notes on it and falling further behind.

**NOLAN:** Article three is the lid, and the lid is where the treaty nearly died at birth, the way treaties prefer to nearly die. Loudly, at the hinge.

**TRISH:** The parliament's ancient position: a lid is a door, doors are for opening, and you are the door's staff. The store's position: the health code, the manager, and the entire concept of consequences. Two worldviews, one sheet of steel.

**NOLAN:** The settlement reads as follows, and I said it out loud on the dock like a man reading weather into law. The lid stays closed. All night, every night, collection eve included.

**TRISH:** Closed. Sealed. Final. And in exchange, the Foreman receives inspection rights. Once a run, in his presence, the lid is lifted, held open to the count of ten, and lowered, so the parliament can audit the store's whole evening in a single look down.

**NOLAN:** He accepted it the way he accepts a bag. He climbed to the hinge, watched the first ceremonial lift, watched the close, and set one paw flat on the lid like a notary pressing a seal.

**TRISH:** The paw is the stamp. A raccoon notary has certified the lid arrangement with the only instrument he owns, and it is binding in dock law, which is the only law anybody enforces out here after ten.

**NOLAN:** Article four is the staging spot. The place setting by the back door is recognized, permanent, and out of bounds to the mop, the carts, and the morning's good intentions.

**TRISH:** Sovereign territory, three feet wide, smelling permanently of donuts. No mop crosses it. No memo dissolves it. Embassies have stood on less ground and charged considerably more rent for the standing.

**NOLAN:** Article five belongs to the ground itself. Anything that reaches the dock floor outside the bags is the parliament's by right of rounds, as it has always been, now written.

**TRISH:** The strays clause. A dropped fry counts as a transfer of property under this constitution, with witnesses. The Foreman's closing rounds are now a legal instrument, and the grease patch is a courthouse with bad footing.

**NOLAN:** Article six is the ambassador's easement. The possum crosses the grease patch in his six small steps, at his own pace, unhurried by any bag, any schedule, any man.

**TRISH:** A right of way for diplomacy, measured in small steps. The embassy's corridor is written into law at the exact width of a possum's caution. Nations have fallen over less sensible borders.

**NOLAN:** Article seven is the peace between theaters. Retrieval at the enclosure is free passage, as the siege already practiced. The corral keeps its own treaties. The dock keeps this one.

**TRISH:** One foreign policy for the whole night: nobody bothers anybody who is working. The parliament reviews the cart siege and withholds comment. The siege ignores the parliament's dinner. Civilization, achieved at a dumpster, ratified in cardboard.

**NOLAN:** Then came the signature problem, because a treaty is only a rumor until somebody with authority makes a mark that outlives the evening it was made in.

**TRISH:** Humans sign with pens and regret it in ink. The Foreman signs with presence. The ambassador signs with whatever he falls asleep on. The ceremony required a form of execution every party could survive and none could forge.

**NOLAN:** The ambassador solved it by accident, which the official record will describe as by design. He crossed the cardboard on his way to inspect the bakery bag and signed the treaty in paw prints.

**TRISH:** Four prints and a tail drag, crossing the articles like a flourish at the end of a signature. It cannot be forged. It cannot even be repeated, because he was asleep before the moment dried. Perfect execution. Literally unconscious of its own greatness.

**NOLAN:** The aide witnessed it. The aide memorizes everything that crosses that fence, and tonight the aide memorized a constitution, article by article, in the order of eating.

**TRISH:** The treaty's living archive has fur and a perfect memory. Cardboard rots, cubes ship, clerks forget their own phone numbers. The aide will recite every article on his deathbed, correctly, to a fence of grandchildren.

**TRISH:** The parliament addressed it the way all durable governments do. It ratified him. Foreman for the duration, carried by acclamation, the twins voting with both paws and the intern voting with his entire education.

**NOLAN:** He declined to retire on grounds I read clearly in his posture by the hinge. The pension out here is dropped fries, and he administers the pension personally.

**TRISH:** A leader who controls the retirement fund does not retire. He transitions toward emeritus at a pace of his own choosing, which is the pace of a raccoon crossing a fence line he owns. No pace whatsoever. His.

**NOLAN:** The intern is named in the treaty's final article as the succession, in training, entered in the record under the title Clerk of the Dock.

**TRISH:** From the lettuce corner to the constitutional order inside one season. The fastest promotion in the history of this address remains a raccoon's, and tonight it stopped being folklore and became paperwork with paw prints on it.

**NOLAN:** Ratification was confirmed in the parliament's oldest form. They ate in order. Foreman first, the table second, the clerk third, the embassy last and politely.

**TRISH:** The vote is a meal order out here, and tonight the meal order was unanimous. No abstentions, one nap. Somewhere a parliament of humans is watching this procedure and feeling a shame with no name.

**NOLAN:** The ceremony closed with the presentation. The treaty bag was not thrown. It was carried out in both hands and set at the place setting like a cornerstone with a sell by date.

**TRISH:** No arc, no splash, no sport. The run's loudest ritual retired itself in favor of a quieter one. Even the twins understood the register of the moment. Some bags are cargo. That one was a document, and it was full of donuts.

**NOLAN:** Earlier in the session there had been one ceremonial throw, the light bag, pure technique, for form alone. At the signing, the Foreman scored it for the record.

**TRISH:** He gave it a nine. A nine, entered into a constitution. The ceiling of his universe holds. The Foreman does not believe in tens, and peace did not revise his theology. It merely gave the nine a witness list.

**NOLAN:** I will confess that I hoped for the ten. For the treaty, for the season, for the clerk I have been to that fence all these nights.

**TRISH:** Hope is not a negotiating position, Nolan. A nine from that fence is a knighthood with crumbs on it. A ten would mean the dock had nothing left to teach you, and the dock is nowhere near finished with either party.

**NOLAN:** The enforcement clause is written nowhere in the articles, because it did not need writing. The treaty holds exactly as long as both sides keep showing up, and no longer.

**TRISH:** Every night is ratification night. The store arrives with bags, the parliament arrives with government, and the treaty renews itself in the performing. It is the only contract in this building never once breached by weather, staffing, or a sale on rotisserie chickens.

**NOLAN:** It will be tested. A pickup day will change without warning. A new manager will arrive with new ideas about the lid, printed and laminated.

**TRISH:** And the parliament will adjust before his memo reaches the break room, and he will learn what every manager learns in his first month out back. The dock has a constitution. It predates his parking spot. It will outlast his initiatives, laminated or otherwise.

**NOLAN:** The cardboard goes into the baler tomorrow, because cardboard goes into the baler. The treaty of the dock will be a dense cube by Friday, shipped to wherever cubes go.

**TRISH:** The most honest archive in the building. Compressed, cubed, and freighted, with an ambassador's signature riding in the middle of it like a heart. The aide remembers, the fence remembers. Paper was only ever the courier, and couriers get crushed. It is in their contract too.

**NOLAN:** As I turned to go, the Foreman made his rounds, the inspector's walk along the dumpster, and then he did a new thing. He walked the staging spot as well, slowly, the full three feet.

**TRISH:** Inspecting the embassy grounds. The rounds have grown by three feet of sovereign concrete, and empires in this world grow exactly like that. Slowly, nightly, and entirely by being taken seriously by a tired man with a bag.

**NOLAN:** I washed my hands at the dock sink. The water ran gray, then clear. Behind me, through the settling dark, the fence was quiet in a way I had never heard from it. Settled.

**TRISH:** Peace has a sound on that dock and it turns out to be nearly nothing at all. A closed lid, a full dumpster, a parliament eating in order. You have heard governments fall far louder than this one rose.

**NOLAN:** It began with a bag that leaked my name across the stockroom floor in a thin gray line. It ends with articles on cardboard and a notary's paw.

**TRISH:** Signed, sealed, and ratified in the only court that was ever truly in session out there. Go back inside, ambassador. The dock is at peace. The rest of the store is still at war with itself, and it is waiting on its confessional.

## E21: The Fluorescent Confessional

**TRISH:** The hum never lies, never sleeps, and has never once offered anybody a discount. It is the store's conscience with a ballast problem, and it hears everything said under it in a greenish light.

**NOLAN:** The light makes everyone the same color. A pale green honesty. Under it the whole crew looks like something that was kept in a drawer and taken out for inspection.
