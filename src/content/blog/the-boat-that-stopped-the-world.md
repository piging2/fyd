In March 2021, a container ship the length of four football fields turned slightly sideways in the Suez Canal and stopped a noticeable fraction of the world economy. The Ever Given, 400 meters long, carrying roughly 18,000 containers, ran aground in a narrow, single-lane stretch of the canal during a sandstorm with strong winds. For six days it sat wedged between the banks while excavators the size of garden sheds (by comparison) dug at its bow and the internet produced, conservatively, ten thousand memes about one small digger doing its best.

The numbers around the blockage became a genre of journalism unto themselves. Lloyd's List estimated that about $9.6 billion worth of trade passed through the canal per day and was therefore delayed; treat that figure as [an informed estimate rather than a measurement](/technology/evidence), but even half of it describes a planetary-scale queue. More than 400 vessels waited by the time the ship was refloated on March 29, helped by dredging, tugboats, and a full moon's tide. Some ships gave up and took the long way around Africa, adding weeks and a small fortune in fuel, a detour that had been obsolete for 150 years until, briefly, it wasn't.

The comedy of the small excavator hides the structural story, which is the one worth keeping. The global supply chain had been optimized, over decades, toward a single property: cost. Ships grew enormous because bigger ships move a box more cheaply. Ports, canals, and schedules co-evolved around the giants. Inventory shrank to just-in-time minimums because warehousing is money asleep. Every individual decision was rational and measurably better than the alternative. The sum of all of them was a system with magnificent efficiency and almost no slack, threaded through a ditch in Egypt wide enough for exactly one giant at a time.

Engineers have a word for the property the system had optimized away: margin. Margin is the part of a design that looks wasteful on every ordinary day and priceless on the one extraordinary day. A canal with more width, a supply chain with buffer stock, a schedule with recovery time, a disk kept below a hard floor: all of them read, in a quarterly review, as inefficiency. All of them are the same idea wearing different clothes. The cost of margin is continuous and visible. The cost of its absence is rare and total, and human organizations are exquisitely built to underweight rare-and-total.

Our factory learned this with a disk instead of a canal, which is why the Ever Given is pinned above the storage desk. We keep a protected reserve of free space that normal work may never allocate, enforced before pressure, not apologized for after. On every ordinary day that reserve is, in the narrow sense, wasted space. On the day a runaway process starts eating the disk, the reserve is [the difference between an incident and an obituary](/technology/continuity): it is where the logs, the tools, and the recovery work live while you fix the thing that is trying to kill the machine. A system with no reserve does not fail gracefully. It fails completely, at the exact moment it needs resources to recover, which is the only moment resources really matter.

There is a second lesson, quieter, about single points of failure that are not components but places. The canal did not malfunction. It functioned exactly as geography allowed, and geography is the one vendor you cannot switch. Every architecture has a Suez: the one bridge, account, person, or credential whose blockage converts local trouble into global queue. The exercise is never to eliminate it; some geography is real. The exercise is to know yours by name, keep the detour chart current, and rehearse the long way around Africa before the wind picks up.

Six days, one ship, one small excavator, and a planet reminded that efficiency is a strategy for ordinary days. The extraordinary days are paid for in margin, bought in advance, or in queue, at whatever the market charges. We choose margin. It photographs terribly. It has never once blocked the canal.

Sources, for the record: the grounding occurred on March 23, 2021, and the Ever Given was refloated on March 29, 2021, per the Suez Canal Authority and contemporaneous reporting. The roughly $9.6 billion per day trade figure is the widely cited Lloyd's List estimate. Vessel queue counts above 400 and the diversion of ships around the Cape of Good Hope were reported by the Associated Press, Reuters, and maritime press at the time.
