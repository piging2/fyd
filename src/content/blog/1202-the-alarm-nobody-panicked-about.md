Seven minutes before the first landing on the Moon, in a cabin the size of a phone booth, an amber light came on and a computer announced that it was in trouble.

Neil Armstrong's voice, when it reached Houston a second and a half later, was almost offensively calm. "Program alarm," he said. Then, four seconds later: "It's a 1202." Buzz Aldrin, beside him, keyed the little display and keyboard unit the astronauts called the DSKY and confirmed it. 1202. Neither man had ever seen the code in training. Neither man knew what it meant. Armstrong, still flying the descent with one part of his mind, asked Houston the only question that mattered: "Give us a reading on the 1202 program alarm."

Eleven seconds after that request, the answer came back up from the Earth, from a 26 year old guidance officer who had been told, in effect, to bet the first Moon landing on his own preparation. "We're Go on that alarm," said Charlie Duke, relaying it. Eagle kept falling toward the Sea of Tranquility, and the computer that had just cried for help kept flying it.

There would be five alarms in all over the next four minutes, and Houston would call "Go" on every one of them, the last three on nothing more than a young engineer in a back room shouting "Same type!" at his console. It is the most famous computer malfunction in history, and the strangest thing about it, the thing worth building an essay around, is that almost everything about it went right. The alarm was not the sound of a system failing. It was the sound of a system doing, under maximum pressure, exactly what its designers had taught it to do. And the humans around it did the same.

To understand why nobody panicked, you have to understand what a 1202 actually was, because the number is widely remembered and widely misunderstood. It was not an error code in the modern sense, not a crash report, not the machine confessing a bug. It was a capacity announcement. The Apollo Guidance Computer ran its work through a piece of software called the Executive, written around a design by J. Halcombe Laning at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory. The Executive was a scheduler with opinions. Every job in the computer carried a priority. The jobs that kept Eagle alive, reading the accelerometers, solving the guidance equations, throttling the descent engine, updating the crew's displays, ran in a master cycle once every two seconds, and they outranked everything else. Lower priority work, displays, radar processing, housekeeping, took what time was left.

When a new job wanted to start, the Executive reserved it a small block of working state. There were two kinds of these blocks: core sets, for ordinary jobs, and VAC areas, short for vector accumulator areas, for jobs doing certain kinds of math. The supply was finite, because everything about that computer was finite. Its permanent memory, core rope, a storage medium quite literally woven by hand, thread by thread through magnetic cores, held 36,864 words. Its working memory held 2,048. Your phone will not deign to notice numbers like these. Into that tiny machine, the team led by Margaret Hamilton at MIT had packed the entire business of flying to the surface of the Moon, and they had done it knowing, with the clarity that only scarcity gives you, that the machine could be asked for more than it had.

So the Executive had a plan for exactly that moment. If a job requested a core set and none was free, the computer raised alarm 1202. If it requested a VAC area and none was free, alarm 1201. Then it did the thing that separates engineering from hope: it restarted itself, on purpose, in a controlled way, in milliseconds. The software restart threw away the unfinished low priority work that was clogging the queues, restored the critical jobs from their protected checkpoints, and carried on flying. It did not even reschedule the bogus radar jobs that had caused the pileup. The alarm, in other words, was the visible tip of a recovery that had already happened by the time the light came on. The computer was not saying "I am broken." It was saying "I received more work than I can schedule, so I have discarded the work that does not matter and protected the work that does. Here is my receipt."

Hold that up against how most software behaves when it is overloaded, in 1969 or now. The usual failure mode is a death spiral: the queue grows, everything slows, the important and the trivial drown together, and the first symptom anyone sees is silence. The Apollo computer failed the other way. It degraded gracefully, loudly, and in public, shedding load in a published order, and it told the crew what it had done in a four digit code. Graceful degradation was not a feature bolted onto that software in a panic. It was the architecture, chosen years earlier by people who assumed the overload would come and refused to let the machine decide, in the moment, what mattered. The deciding had been done in advance, in calm rooms, by engineers. That is the entire story, and everything else is the cast proving it worked.

Now for the cast, because the other half of the miracle happened on the ground, in Houston, in the seconds after Armstrong asked his question.

In the front room of Mission Control, in the row of consoles the controllers called the Trench, sat Steve Bales, call sign GUIDO, the guidance officer, 26 years old. His job, at that moment, was to be the world's foremost authority on whether Eagle's computer could be trusted, and to render that judgment in the time it takes a falling spacecraft to fall. Bales did not render it from memory. He asked the back room, and in the back room sat Jack Garman, 24 years old, a computer specialist who supported the guidance position, with a piece of paper under the plexiglass on his console that he had written by hand, in ballpoint, weeks before.

The paper existed because of a rehearsal that had gone badly. Late in the training program, Garman himself had suggested to the simulation supervisor, Dick Koos, that the controllers be tested on their reactions to computer alarms. Koos obliged, and in the final full simulation, with another crew in the simulator, a program alarm of this same family appeared and Bales called an abort. It was the wrong call. The alarm, it turned out, was one the computer could recover from on its own, and the landing, had it been real, would have been thrown away for nothing. Flight director Gene Kranz was, by all accounts including his own, incandescent. His remedy was characteristically unglamorous. Every controller would write down every computer alarm that could possibly occur, and next to each, what the correct response was, and learn it cold. Garman did the work for the computer alarms. Against 1201 and 1202, his note said, in effect: the computer is shedding work and recovering; if the alarm is occasional, you may continue; if it is continuous, you have a real problem.

So when Armstrong said "1202," Garman did not have to think, and that is precisely the point. Thinking had been done, weeks earlier, at a desk, with time to check. Bales later admitted that Garman had said it was fine before Bales himself could even remember which family the code belonged to. Bales turned to the flight director and said the bravest sentence of his life: "We're Go on that, Flight." Kranz accepted it. Duke sent it up. The whole transaction, from a pilot asking a question over a quarter million miles of vacuum to the answer coming back, took about eleven seconds of loop time plus the light travel, and it rested on a ballpoint list taped under plastic by the youngest man in the chain.

The alarms were not finished. Forty seconds later a second 1202 arrived, and Bales and Garman went through the same drill: same type, intermittent, computer recovering, Go. Then the descent moved into its final program, the approach phase, Eagle pitched upright so the crew could finally see where they were going, and at about 3,000 feet the computer produced a rapid cluster: a 1201 and two more 1202s, three alarms in under forty seconds. This time the exchange on the ground loop was shorter, because the system of humans had now been trained by the event itself. Garman, by his own account, was by then yelling. "Same type!" he shouted. Bales echoed it to the flight director. Duke, to the crew: "Roger. 1201 alarm. We're Go. Same type. We're Go." Notice what had been built, in layers, over years, by then: a machine that announced its own degradation in a vocabulary of codes, a written table translating those codes into decisions, and a chain of people who had rehearsed trusting the table. The alarm the crew understood, because Houston understood it, because a young engineer had been ordered to understand all of them before the day came. An alarm you understand is information. An alarm you do not understand is just fear with a light on it.

And what, in the end, had the computer been complaining about? Something almost insultingly small. Eagle carried two radars. The landing radar, which measured height and speed over the surface, was essential and behaved. The rendezvous radar existed to find the command module again after takeoff, and the checklist had it powered and warmed during the descent, mode switch in SLEW or AUTO, so that it would be ready if the landing had to be aborted in a hurry. It was tracking nothing. But its electronics talked to the computer through devices called coupling data units, and here a gremlin had been living quietly for years. The radar's angle sensors were excited by an 800 cycle power supply; the coupling units worked from a different 800 cycle supply. Same frequency, but the two were not locked in phase, and whether they drifted into the bad relationship was a coin flip at every power up. When the coin came up wrong, the coupling units read the stationary antenna as if it were whipping around impossibly fast, and they began ordering the computer to update the radar's angle counters at up to 6,400 increments a second, on each of two channels. Every increment stole a memory cycle from the running programs. The total theft, the post flight investigation concluded, was about 15 percent of the computer. 

Fifteen percent. The descent software had been built with a time margin, and it had been tested to tolerate losing roughly a tenth of the machine. The radar, doing nothing, tracking nothing, in a mode chosen for prudence, took a sixth. Jobs that should have finished lingered; new copies of periodic jobs started before old copies had ended; the reservations piled up until the Executive hit the bottom of its core sets and its VAC areas and did the one thing it was built to do in that corner. The saboteur had not even been secret. Engineers had seen the rendezvous radar interface misbehave during testing of an earlier lunar module years before, on a lightly loaded computer, where it caused nothing worse than untidy telemetry. At descent loading, the same untidiness became five alarms in four minutes. There is no villain in this story anywhere. There is a switch set the way the checklist said, a hardware quirk documented by people who could not know where it would matter, and a software team that had quietly assumed the worst about their own machine and armored it accordingly.

The landing itself almost crowds the alarms out of memory, which is unfair to both. Coming down through the final thousand feet, Armstrong saw that the computer's chosen spot sat in a field of boulders around a crater, and he took semi manual control and flew Eagle onward, level, hunting for clear ground, while Aldrin read out altitude and descent rate in his steady litany and the cabin filled, at the end, with dust blown sideways by the engine. Houston's contributions shrank to the only numbers that mattered now, the fuel calls, passed from flight dynamics officer Bob Carlton's figures through Duke: "60 seconds." Then: "30 seconds." Armstrong set Eagle down at 20:17 UTC with, by the readings the crew and controllers had that day, roughly 25 seconds of flying time left in the tanks before the abort limit; post flight analysis, untangling the sloshing propellant and the gauge behavior, put the true figure closer to 45 to 50 seconds. Either way you reckon it, the margin was measured in heartbeats, and the crew had spent four of the descent's final minutes being told, correctly, by their own computer that it was out of room. They landed anyway, upright, in one piece, on the first try, with a computer that had restarted itself five times on the way down and had never once stopped flying the spacecraft.

Our own studio runs [small software workers](/technology/agents) around the clock, and the Apollo pattern is taped to the wall of every design discussion we have, because it is the whole discipline in one afternoon. Every worker we run carries a priority, a budget, and a prewritten answer to the question of what it may drop when the queue exceeds the budget. Every alarm our system can raise exists in a written table with the correct human response next to it, and the table was written, in the Garman manner, on a calm day, after a rehearsal in which somebody made the wrong call and we made him write the list. And every failure report is expected to arrive as [a receipt](/technology/evidence): what was shed, what was protected, what state the critical work is in. Activity is not output, and an alarm is not output either. An alarm is a message from the past, from the engineers who designed the degradation, to the operators living through it. It is only useful if somebody wrote down, in advance, what it means.

So here is the shape of it, the thing July 20, 1969 proved at the highest stakes our species has yet managed. You do not rise to the occasion; you fall to the level of your design, and of your preparation. The computer that landed on the Moon was, by any modern measure, pathetically weak: two thousand words of working memory, a program woven into rope, a top speed a pocket calculator would be embarrassed by. It did not survive the rendezvous radar because it was powerful. It survived because Laning and Hamilton's engineers had decided, years before, in writing, in the architecture itself, what the machine would protect and what it would throw away when it could not do everything, and because Kranz's controllers had decided, weeks before, in ballpoint, what each cry for help would mean. The falling spacecraft met a falling back through layer after layer of decisions made by people who were not frightened, because they were not yet in the frightening moment. That is what the calm voices on the loop actually are. They are the sound of decisions arriving from the past, right on time.

The most important sound of the twentieth century may not be "The Eagle has landed." It may be the quieter one, four minutes earlier: a computer telling the truth about its own limits, and a room full of people who had prepared so well that the truth did not scare them.
