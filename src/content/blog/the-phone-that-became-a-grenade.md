On the morning of October 5, 2016, Southwest Airlines flight 994 was sitting at the gate in Louisville, Kentucky, preparing to fly to Baltimore, when a passenger did exactly what the cabin crew had asked him to do. Brian Green powered his phone down and put it in his pocket. Then he heard a pop, felt the heat, and watched what he later described as thick grey-green angry smoke pour out of his clothes.

Green dropped the phone on the cabin floor, where it burned through the carpet and scorched the subfloor of the aircraft. The flight was evacuated through the main cabin door. Nobody was hurt. The plane never left the gate, which is the best possible version of a fire on an airplane, and it was still a fire on an airplane, caused by a phone, in a passenger's pocket, while the phone was off.

Here is the part that turned a bad month into a historic one. Green's phone was not one of the defective originals. It was a replacement Samsung Galaxy Note 7, picked up from an AT&T store on September 21, issued under Samsung's recall program precisely because it was supposed to be safe. It had the green battery icon Samsung used to mark the good ones. Its box had the black square symbol that marked it as a post-recall unit. Every signal Samsung had invented to say "this one is fine" was present and correct, and the phone caught fire anyway, in the one environment on Earth where a small fire stops being a product problem and becomes a federal case.

Within nine days, the United States Department of Transportation had issued an emergency order banning the Galaxy Note 7 from air transportation entirely: not on your person, not in your carry-on, not in your checked luggage, not as cargo. Airlines were required to announce the ban before boarding and to deny boarding to anyone carrying one. Flight crews spent the autumn of 2016 reciting a specific consumer product's name into cabin loudspeakers like a tiny celebrity with a no-fly list. The Note 7 had achieved something no other phone ever has, before or since. It was classified, by name, as a forbidden hazardous material.

To understand how a flagship phone got itself individually banned from the sky, you have to go back to the design brief, because the Note 7's real defect was not in either battery. It was in the sentence the batteries were asked to live inside: thinner phone, bigger battery, same space.

### Defect one: the battery that did not fit its own house

The Galaxy Note 7 launched on August 19, 2016, and by the standards of the day it was a genuinely impressive object: a 5.7 inch curved display, an iris scanner, a stylus, and a 3,500 milliamp-hour battery packed into a body that reviewers praised for how slim it felt. That last pairing, big battery and slim body, is where the trouble lived. A lithium-ion battery is not a static block. Its electrode assembly, a layered roll of positive electrode, negative electrode, and a thin separator keeping them apart, swells and contracts as the cell charges and discharges. It needs room to breathe. In the Note 7's first battery, which the investigation would later call Battery A, it did not reliably have that room.

Samsung's January 2017 investigation, run with independent organizations UL, Exponent, and TÜV Rheinland, concluded that Battery A's cell pouch provided inadequate volume to accommodate the electrode assembly. The assembly was compressed, and the negative electrode deflected, bent, consistently in the corner of the cell. Bent electrodes stress the separator, the only thing standing between the positive and negative sides of a device whose entire job is to keep those sides apart until asked. The separator in this design was thin to begin with. Add repeated mechanical stress from normal charging cycles and, in some cells, insulation tape that was missing or insufficient over the positive tab, and you have multiple paths to the same destination: an internal short circuit, rapid heating, and what battery engineers call thermal runaway, which is the technical term for the moment a battery stops being a battery and becomes a small, self-sustaining fire.

The fires began within weeks of launch. By September 1, Samsung had received 35 reports of battery incidents worldwide. On September 2, the company halted sales and announced a global recall of 2.5 million phones. Its diagnosis, announced with confidence, was a battery cell issue confined to one supplier. The fix followed logically from the diagnosis: keep the phone, swap the battery supplier. Replacement Note 7s would use cells from the other supplier, the one whose batteries had shown no problems. The replacements got the green battery icon and the black square on the box so everyone, customers, carriers, and flight crews, could tell the safe phones from the dangerous ones at a glance.

On September 15, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission made the first recall official in the United States: about 1 million phones, with Samsung reporting 92 cases of batteries overheating in the U.S., including 26 reports of burns and 55 reports of property damage, among them fires in cars and a garage. The framing was orderly and reassuring. A bad batch from a bad supplier, identified, isolated, and being replaced with verified-safe units. This is the part of the story where the recall was working, right up until the moment the replacement phones started catching fire too.

### The replacement fiasco: a second launch with worse press

The replacements had a problem that the diagnosis had never looked for, because the diagnosis had already concluded. Battery B, the cell from the second supplier, was a different battery with a different design and, as it turned out, a different defect. Samsung's investigation later found that Battery B's original cells showed no deficiency. The defect arrived with the ramp. When Samsung suddenly needed millions of Battery B cells to replace the recalled fleet, production sped up, and quality slipped in a specific, lethal way: the ultrasonic welding that attached the positive tab left abnormally high burrs, sharp protrusions tall enough, under the normal swelling and contraction of charge cycles, to be driven through the insulation tape and the separator and into direct contact with the negative electrode. Some cells were assembled without the protective insulation tape over the tab at all. Internal short circuit. Thermal runaway. Same fire, new cause, in the phones that were supposed to end the fires.

Read that sequence slowly, because it is the whole lesson wearing a trench coat. The replacement battery was not validated to the standard its job required. It was validated to the standard of "not the battery we just blamed." Samsung checked that Battery B did not have Battery A's problem, shipped it into a recall, and only discovered Battery B's own problem the way its customers did: in the field, at scale, in public, once on a parked airliner in Louisville.

The end came fast after that. Replacement phones were reported smoking and burning in the first days of October. On October 10, Samsung halted sales and production while it investigated. On October 11, six days after flight 994, the company permanently discontinued the Galaxy Note 7, less than two months after launch. On October 13, the CPSC expanded the U.S. recall to about 1.9 million phones, covering every Note 7 ever sold in the United States including all the replacements, with 96 U.S. [overheating reports on the record](/technology/events). On October 14, the DOT, FAA, and PHMSA issued the emergency ban. On the same day, Samsung put a number on the damage: the discontinuation would cost roughly $3 billion more over the current and next quarters, bringing the total cost of the recall to at least $5.3 billion. That figure does not count the thing Samsung's executives were presumably doing the math on at night, which is how many years of brand trust you can buy back, and at what exchange rate.

And still the phones were out there. Samsung pushed software updates that capped charging, then further updates that reduced the battery to a paperweight with a screen. Airports set up exchange desks. The announcements before boarding went on for months, a little liturgy of corporate failure recited at every gate in America: if you are carrying a Samsung Galaxy Note 7, you may not fly with it, you may not charge it, you may not put it in your checked bag. Laptops could fly. Lighters faced restrictions, but a phone had joined the category of things the federal government names individually, a category otherwise populated by materials with warning diamonds on the crate.

### The investigation: how to do the second one properly

To Samsung's credit, and this matters for the lesson, the investigation that followed was not a press release with a lab coat on. The company put roughly 700 researchers and engineers on the problem, built a large-scale test facility, and tested more than 200,000 fully assembled devices and more than 30,000 batteries, deliberately replicating the failures. It hired three independent organizations, UL, Exponent, and TÜV Rheinland, to reach their own conclusions. On January 23, 2017, in Seoul, Samsung stood up and published the answer, including the parts that indicted its own process.

The answer was that there was no single answer. The phone's hardware and software were exonerated; the fires came from the batteries, plural, with separate causes, plural. Battery A: a design failure, a pouch too small for its contents, electrodes deflecting in the corner, a separator too thin to forgive it. Battery B: a manufacturing failure, welding burrs and missing tape, introduced when production ramped to meet the recall's demand. And hovering over both, Samsung's own admission, worth quoting in spirit if not in letter: the company set the target specifications, took responsibility for the failure to identify and verify the battery problems before launch, and conceded it had not thoroughly vetted the replacement batteries either. The corrective program that followed, a multi-layer safety protocol and an 8-Point Battery Safety Check applied to every battery Samsung ships, is what validation depth looks like after it has been priced by reality.

Notice the asymmetry, because it is the most instructive number in the whole saga. The investigation that finally produced the truth tested 200,000 devices. The recall that shipped the second defective battery to millions of people was, in effect, tested by the public. The difference between those two numbers is not budget. Samsung had the budget the whole time. The difference is sequence: validation performed before the claim, or validation performed by the claim's victims. One of those is engineering. The other is a recall with better marketing.

### The lesson: a swap is not a fix

Here is the engineering principle the Note 7 bought for $5.3 billion, available to the rest of us at a steep discount.

A recall that swaps one unvalidated component for another unvalidated component is not a fix. It is a second launch, with worse press, conducted in front of an audience that has already been burned once, literally in 26 documented cases in the first recall alone. The first failure cost Samsung a product launch. The second failure, the identical failure arriving in the packaging marked safe, cost Samsung the product, the recall's credibility, $5.3 billion, and a permanent place in every pre-flight announcement in America. Blast radius is not determined by the size of the component. A battery cell is a few grams of layered foil. Its blast radius, under the wrong validation regime, turned out to be an entire product line, a government ban, and a decade of jokes that still open every article about phone safety. Validation depth has to match blast radius, and blast radius has to be assessed by what the component can take down, not by what the component weighs.

There is a subtler failure underneath the technical one, and it is the one worth taping to the wall. Samsung's first diagnosis was not stupid. Battery A really was defective. The failure was that a true diagnosis of the first fire was treated as a complete theory of the system. "Supplier A's battery is defective" silently became "everything else about this phone is fine," including a second battery that had never been asked to survive at recall scale, in a phone whose design brief, thinner body, bigger battery, same space, was applying pressure to every cell anyone installed in it. In our own studio we run a version of this rule that sounds small and is not: [a claim is not a fact](/technology/evidence), and a replaced part is not a verified part. The replacement inherits none of the original's evidence. It starts at zero, and it earns trust the same way the original was supposed to, by being tested to the depth its failure would demand.

Every engineering organization has a Note 7 in its future, scaled to its own ambitions: a component swapped under time pressure, a fix verified against the old failure instead of against the world, a green icon painted on a box to tell everyone, including the people who painted it, that this one is safe. The icon is the tell. Certainty that arrives before the testing does is not confidence. It is decoration. Samsung eventually did the testing, all 200,000 devices of it, and published what it found, and built the checklist that should have existed in August into a permanent institution. That is the genuinely respectable ending, and it arrived one product too late.

The Galaxy Note 7 remains the only phone in history famous enough to be banned from the sky by name, a celebrity with a no-fly list and a $5.3 billion bar tab, and the moral of its short, bright life fits in one sentence: the fix is not the thing you ship instead, the fix is the proof you run first.
